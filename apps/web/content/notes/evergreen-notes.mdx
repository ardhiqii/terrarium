The term *evergreen notes* comes from Andy Matuschak's note-taking practice. The idea is simple but the implications are large: **write notes that last**.

## What makes a note evergreen?

Most notes are ephemeral. They capture the context of a moment — a meeting summary, a quick reference, a reaction to something you read. These decay quickly. Evergreen notes are different.

An evergreen note:

- Is written in **your own words**, not copied from a source
- Makes a **single, specific claim** (not a topic dump)
- Is **titled as an assertion**, not a question or category
- Is **linked** to related ideas
- Gets **updated** as your thinking evolves

## The difference between topic notes and concept notes

A topic note is "Machine Learning". It's a folder pretending to be a note.

An evergreen note is "Gradient descent is a hill-climbing algorithm running in reverse." That's a claim you can reason from and connect to other claims.

The goal is to build a collection of claims — not a collection of topics.

## Why this connects to the garden metaphor

In [[What Is a Terrarium?]], I described how notes exist in different stages of growth. Evergreen notes are the endpoint — ideas developed enough to be useful and stable enough to build on.

Seedlings become evergreen through:

1. **Revisiting** — coming back to a note after a few days and asking "do I still believe this?"
2. **Connecting** — finding links to other notes that sharpen or challenge the idea
3. **Compressing** — cutting until only the essential claim remains

## Practical tip

When you save a new note, ask: *"What does this claim?"*

If you can't answer in one sentence, the note probably isn't atomic enough yet. Split it, or let it stay a seedling until it clarifies.
