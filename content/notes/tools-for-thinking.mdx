Not productivity hacks. Not apps that promise to change your life. Just things that have actually changed how I think, and have stayed useful over time.

## Writing as thinking

The most reliable thinking tool I have is writing. Not writing *about* thinking — writing *as* thinking. When something is unclear, writing forces structure. The blank page resists vague ideas.

This is why [[Evergreen Notes]] matter: writing a claim in your own words is harder than highlighting a paragraph. That friction is doing work.

## The constraint of one tab

Browser tab proliferation is deferred decision-making. Every open tab is a question you haven't answered yet. Closing them forces a decision: is this important enough to save explicitly? Usually the answer is no.

One useful practice: when you're done with a research session, close everything. If something was important, you'll remember to look for it again. If you don't remember, it wasn't that important.

## Spaced repetition for ideas (not just facts)

Spaced repetition (Anki, etc.) is usually associated with memorizing facts. But you can use the same principle for ideas — revisiting a note at increasing intervals lets you test whether it still holds up.

The key question on review: *"Do I still believe this? What would make me update it?"*

## Rubber duck debugging, but for decisions

When stuck on a decision, explain the situation out loud as if to someone who knows nothing about it. The act of making your assumptions legible usually reveals where the confusion is.

You don't need an actual rubber duck. A blank document works fine.

## Maps of content

When a topic accumulates more than a handful of notes, create a map of content (MOC) — a note that links to all the relevant notes on a topic, with a short description of how they relate.

MOCs are not folders. They can overlap, evolve, and be linked from other notes. They're the connective tissue of a [[What Is a Digital Garden?|garden]].
